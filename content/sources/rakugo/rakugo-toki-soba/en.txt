On Edo-period nights, soba sellers carried their stalls on their shoulders and sold noodles around town. ‖ 〜を担いで: "carrying ~ on the shoulder, and…". 売り歩く = 売る + 歩く: to walk around selling, like a street vendor. 〜ていました describes an ongoing habit in the past.
Today, it would be something like a ramen stall that stays open late at night. ‖ 今で言えば: "in today's terms". 〜のようなものです: "it's something like ~", a handy way to compare.
Also, in the Edo period people counted the hours differently from today. ‖ 数え方 = 数える + 方: "way of counting". Verb stem + 方 = how to do it (食べ方, 読み方).
Around 10 p.m. was called "Yotsu" (four), and around midnight was called "Kokonotsu" (nine). ‖ AをBと呼ぶ: "to call A B". 〜のです gives background. 四つ and 九つ are Edo-period names of hours, not ordinary counting.
If you remember this, today's story will be much more fun. ‖ 覚えておく: "keep in mind (for later)". 〜ておく = do something in advance. 〜と: "if/when ~, then".
On a cold winter night, a man called out to a soba seller who happened to pass by. ‖ 通りかかる: "to happen to pass by". 声をかける: "to call out to, to speak to" — very common in Japan (店員に声をかける).
Hey, soba man. What have you got? ‖ 〜んだい is a casual Edo-style question used by men. Today you would politely say 何がありますか.
Yes sir, we have Hanamaki and Shippoku. ‖ へい: old merchant's "yes, sir". 〜でございます: very polite humble form of です, still heard in hotels and department stores.
Then one Shippoku. Make it hot. ‖ 熱くする: "to make hot". い-adjective minus い + く + する = make something ~. 〜てくれ is a casual male request.
Oh, nice signboard. A picture of an arrow hitting the target, huh? ‖ 的に矢が当たる: "an arrow hits the target". 〜ている describes the state in the picture. He starts flattering the seller.
It says "Atariya" (the Hit Shop)? That's a lucky sign. ‖ 当たり: "a hit" (as in a prize or a target). 縁起がいい: "auspicious". Japanese people care about luck, e.g. choosing good days or gifts.
Thank you very much, sir. ‖ ありがとうございます is the standard polite thank you. The seller is pleased by the praise.
It's chilly tonight. On a night like this, hot soba is the best. ‖ 冷える: "to get cold" (cold that seeps in). 〜が一番だ: "~ is the best". A drawn-out ねえ shows feeling.
Here you are, sorry to keep you waiting. ‖ お待ちどおさま: said when serving, "sorry for the wait". Restaurants today usually say お待たせしました.
Oh, that was fast. Edo folks hate being kept waiting. ‖ 待たされる: causative-passive, "to be made to wait". 江戸っ子: a true Edo native, famous for being impatient and brisk.
Disposable chopsticks, huh. Chopsticks that no one has used yet feel nice. ‖ 割り箸: wooden chopsticks you split apart, used once. 気持ちがいい: "feels pleasant" — used for cleanliness, a cool breeze, a hot spring.
The bowl is nice too. What a pretty pattern. ‖ どんぶり: a large bowl (used for gyūdon, katsudon). Short, rapid compliments are his technique.
The man took a sip of the hot broth. ‖ つゆ: the broth for soba or udon. 一口: "one bite, one sip". 飲む is also used for soup.
Mm, good stock. You don't skimp on the bonito flakes. ‖ だし: Japanese soup stock. けちる: "to be stingy, to skimp". 〜ていないね praises: "you're not skimping".
The noodles are thin and firm, too. And this chikuwa. ‖ 腰がある: (noodles) "firm and springy" — a common compliment for udon and soba. 〜くて links two adjectives.
You cut it this thick? That makes me happy. ‖ 〜てある: a state someone deliberately created (it has been cut). こんなに: "this much". ちくわ is a tube-shaped fish cake.
Yes sir, cutting it thick is our pride. ‖ うち: "our place, our shop". 〜が自慢です: "~ is our pride". Ending with 〜でして is a modest trailing-off used by sellers.
The man finished eating in no time and asked. ‖ あっという間に: "in an instant". 食べ終わる: finish eating. Verb stem + 終わる = finish doing.
Thanks for the meal. How much? ‖ ごちそうさま: said after eating; in Japan you also say it when paying at a restaurant. いくら: "how much (money)".
That will be sixteen mon, sir. ‖ 文 (mon): an Edo-period coin unit. Sixteen mon was the famous price of a bowl of soba then.
I only have small coins. Hold out your hand. I'll count them as I give them. ‖ 細かいお金: "small change" (still used: 細かいのありますか). 〜しかない: "have only ~". 〜ながら: while doing.
One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight. ‖ Old Japanese counting: ひい, ふう, みい… (like ひとつ, ふたつ, みっつ). This is the key moment — listen closely.
What hour is it now? ‖ 何時 (なんどき): how Edo people asked the time. Today you say 今、何時ですか (なんじ).
Yes sir, it's the Ninth Hour. ‖ 九つで: trailing form of 九つでございます. The seller answers the time, but "nine" slips into the count.
Ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen. Thanks. ‖ He continues from "ten", skipping nine, so he pays only 15 mon. ごちそうさん is a casual ごちそうさま.
The man quickly went home. ‖ さっさと: "briskly, without lingering". 帰っていく: going away from the narrator's view.
Someone had been watching all this from the shadows. It was Yotarō. ‖ 陰から: "from a hidden spot". じっと見る: "to stare intently". Yotarō is rakugo's classic lovable fool.
That's strange. That guy praised the soba so much. And the way he counted money was odd too. ‖ ずいぶん: "quite, a lot". 〜ていたな: talking to oneself, recalling. 変だ: "strange".
Yotarō counted again, bending his fingers. ‖ 指を折る: "to fold fingers" to count, the Japanese counting gesture (starting from the thumb). 〜てみる: try doing.
Eight, nine, ten… huh? ‖ あれ？: what you say when you notice something odd. Very common in daily life.
He asked the time right at "nine," so he paid one mon less! ‖ 〜たから: "because (he) did ~". 少なく払う: "to pay less". い-adjective → adverb with 〜く.
Clever! All right, I'll try it too. ‖ うまい: "skillful, clever" (also "tasty"). 〜てみよう: volitional of 〜てみる, "let's try".
The next day, Yotarō prepared small coins and left home early. ‖ 用意する: "to get ready". 早めに: "a bit early". 〜め on adjectives: a little ~ (多め, 少なめ).
He had thought about nothing else all day, and couldn't wait until night. ‖ 一日中: "all day long". 〜ばかり: "nothing but ~". 待ちきれない: "can't wait". 〜のです explains the reason.
After walking a while, he saw a different soba stall. ‖ しばらく: "for a while". 〜と: "when ~, (he found)". 見える: "to be visible".
Hey there, soba lady. One Shippoku, please. ‖ おーい: calling out from a distance. Yotarō copies yesterday's man exactly.
Yes, yes, just a moment. ‖ はいはい: saying "yes" twice sounds a bit offhand. 〜てね: a soft, friendly request.
Nice signboard… huh, there's no signboard. ‖ Sentence-final や: a mild exclamation, talking to oneself. His flattery plan starts to fall apart.
The strong wind blew it away yesterday. ‖ 飛んでいっちゃった = 飛んでいってしまった: "it flew away (sadly)". 〜ちゃう is the spoken form of 〜てしまう.
I see. Then the chopsticks… oh, the tips of these chopsticks are split. ‖ 割れている: "is split/cracked" (state). おや: a mild "oh?".
That's because I wash them and use them again and again. ‖ 何度も: "many times". These are reused chopsticks — the opposite of yesterday's fresh ones.
And the bowl… the rim is chipped. ‖ 欠ける: "to be chipped". ふち: "rim, edge". なあ: a sighing exclamation.
It's old, but it still works. Here you go, sorry for the wait. ‖ 使える: potential form of 使う, "can be used". お待たせ: short for お待たせしました.
Yotarō took a sip of the broth. ‖ This repeats yesterday's scene so the listener can compare.
Ugh, too salty! Add some hot water. ‖ しょっぱい: "salty" (Kansai people often say からい). お湯: "hot water". 足す: "to add".
The noodles are thick and mushy, too. And the chikuwa… huh, there isn't any. ‖ ぶよぶよ: mimetic word for "soggy, flabby". Japanese has many such sound-symbolic words.
Look carefully. It's at the bottom of the bowl, isn't it? ‖ よく見て: "look closely". 〜でしょう with rising tone: "right?", slightly annoyed here.
So thin. I can see right through the chikuwa to the other side. ‖ 透けて見える: "to see through". 向こう: "the other side". A typical rakugo exaggeration.
Yotarō couldn't even eat half. But the important part starts here. ‖ 半分も〜ない: "not even half". 大事なのは: "what matters is".
How much? I've got small coins. Hold out your hand. ‖ Yotarō repeats yesterday's man word for word.
One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight. What hour is it now? ‖ The same question — but this time it is still early.
It's the Fourth Hour. ‖ 四つ: around 10 p.m. Because he came out too early, it isn't "nine" yet.
Five, six, seven, eight… ‖ Yotarō continues from "five" after four. This is the punch line (ochi) of the story.
Yotarō ended up paying four mon too much. ‖ 〜も stresses a large amount ("as many as four"). 〜てしまいました: did something regrettable.
Because he went out too early, he overpaid for terrible soba. ‖ 〜すぎる: "too ~". 〜せいで: "because of ~" (bad result). 余計に: "extra, more than needed".
If you copy someone, you have to think carefully about why it worked for them, don't you. ‖ まねをする: imitate. 〜なら: if ~. 〜なければなりません: must ~.
That was "Toki Soba," a rakugo story that made Edo people roar with laughter. ‖ 落語 (rakugo): a comic storytelling art by one seated performer, from the Edo period, still performed at 寄席 (yose) theaters.
The next story is about young men telling each other what they fear, and a manjū bun. ‖ 言い合う: "to say to each other". 〜合う = do together (話し合う, 助け合う).
